Why a higher Black Friday ROAS doesn't mean profit

A 5× Black Friday ROAS looks impressive until you calculate contribution margin, shipping costs and discount depth. Learn why revenue return alone hides whether you made any profit.

You're chasing a brilliant Black Friday ROAS (return on ad spend, meaning the revenue you earn for every pound spent on ads), watching it climb higher and higher — but when the dust settles, your profit is smaller than expected. It sounds backwards, yet it happens more often than you'd think. By the end of this post, you'll understand why a higher Black Friday ROAS doesn't guarantee more money in the bank, and what numbers you should watch instead to protect your bottom line.

In short

  • ROAS measures revenue, not profit — a strong Black Friday ROAS can still lose money if your contribution margin (what's left after product costs, shipping, and fees) is too thin.
  • Deep discounts double your break-even ROAS — a 40 percent Black Friday discount cuts your margin in half, so you need twice the ROAS just to cover ad spend.
  • New customers acquired on sale often cost more than they're worth — first-time buyers during heavy promotions tend to return products more, rarely buy again at full price, and deliver lower lifetime value.

What contribution margin tells you that ROAS hides

ROAS shows you how much revenue comes back for every pound you spend on ads. If you spend £1,000 and generate £5,000 in sales, your ROAS is 5. That looks brilliant on paper. The trouble is, ROAS counts every pound of revenue equally, whether you keep most of it as profit or hand nearly all of it back out to cover what the sale actually cost you.

Contribution margin is the money left over after you subtract the variable costs tied directly to each sale. Start with your product price, then take away the cost of goods sold (what you paid your supplier or manufacturer), shipping to the customer, payment processing fees, and any discount you offered. What remains is your contribution margin, the amount available to cover your ad spend and fixed overheads before you see profit.

Imagine an online shop selling trainers at £100 each. The cost of goods is £40, shipping costs £5, the payment processor takes £2, and you offer no discount. Your contribution margin is £100 minus £40 minus £5 minus £2, which equals £53. Now picture the same shop running a 40 percent Black Friday sale. The trainers sell for £60, but all the other costs stay the same. Your new contribution margin is £60 minus £40 minus £5 minus £2, only £13. The revenue per sale has dropped by 40 percent, but your margin has collapsed by 75 percent.

That shrinking margin is why a strong ROAS during a heavy promotion can still drain your account. If you need £13 of margin to recover a £15 cost per acquisition, you lose £2 on every sale, no matter how impressive the headline revenue number looks. How deep Black Friday discounts change your break-even ROAS

A 40 percent discount does more than cut your ticket price. It slashes the contribution margin you have left to pay for ads, and that means the ROAS you need just to break even on ad spend can double overnight. Campaigns that hit your target ROAS during normal trading weeks can quietly lose money the moment you switch on a deep Black Friday promotion.

The maths behind break-even ROAS

Break-even ROAS is the point where your ad spend exactly equals your contribution margin. If your margin per sale is £50 and you spend £50 acquiring that customer, you break even at a ROAS of 1. To find your break-even ROAS, divide 1 by your contribution margin as a percentage of revenue.

Take the trainer example from earlier. At full price, each £100 sale leaves you with £53 of margin, so your margin is 53 percent of revenue. Your break-even ROAS is 1 divided by 0.53, which equals 1.89. Any ROAS above 1.89 makes money; anything below loses it.

Now apply the 40 percent Black Friday discount. Revenue per sale drops to £60, and margin falls to £13, only 22 percent of revenue. Your new break-even ROAS is 1 divided by 0.22, which equals 4.55. You need a ROAS of 4.55 just to cover your ad spend, more than double the 1.89 you needed before the sale started. If your campaign delivers a ROAS of 3 — strong by most standards — you're still losing £2.40 on every sale.

This is why chasing the same ROAS target during Black Friday that worked in October can drain your account. The discount changes the economics, and your bidding strategy needs to account for it. Why new-customer acquisition costs more than it looks on Black Friday

A strong Black Friday ROAS often hides a painful truth: many of the customers you acquire during heavy-discount periods never come back, and when they do return, they expect the same low prices. That first sale might look profitable on your dashboard, but if the customer demands a discount every time or simply disappears after one purchase, the lifetime value can fall well below what you spent to win them.

First-time buyers during sales have lower lifetime value

Shoppers hunting for Black Friday bargains are often deal-seekers, not loyal fans of your brand. They buy because the price is right, not because they love what you sell. When the sale ends and prices return to normal, a large share of these customers vanish. In the accounts we manage, we often see return rates spike during promotional periods, and repeat-purchase rates drop compared to customers acquired at full price.

Imagine you spend £50 to acquire a customer who buys once for £60 during your Black Friday sale, giving you a ROAS of 1.2. If that customer never returns, your total revenue from them is £60. After you subtract product cost, shipping, and the discount, you might have £13 of margin left, meaning you lost £37 overall. A customer acquired at full price for the same £50 who goes on to make three purchases over the next year can deliver ten times the profit, even though the first-sale ROAS looked identical.

Measuring profitability over twelve months, not one weekend

Black Friday ROAS becomes meaningful only when you track what happens in the months that follow. Calculate the average order value, purchase frequency, and gross margin of customers acquired during the sale, then compare them to customers won outside promotional windows. If your Black Friday cohort generates 30 percent less revenue over a year and returns products twice as often, the campaign that delivered a ROAS of 4 in November can still destroy profit by March.

This is why we measure contribution margin and lifetime value together, not revenue alone. A lower ROAS on full-price traffic can produce healthier long-term returns than a high ROAS built on one-time bargain hunters.

Common mistakes

  • Optimising Google Ads to a ROAS target without adjusting for contribution margin. If your margin drops from 30 percent to 15 percent during Black Friday, your break-even ROAS doubles, but many advertisers leave their target ROAS unchanged and lose money on every sale. Recalculate your target based on the actual margin each product delivers after the discount, then adjust your Smart Bidding goal or manual bids to match.
  • Treating all revenue equally when new and returning customers have different profitability. A £100 sale to a first-time bargain hunter who never returns is worth far less than a £100 sale to a repeat customer, yet most ROAS reports lump them together. Segment your campaigns or audiences by customer type, and bid more aggressively for high-lifetime-value segments even if the immediate ROAS looks lower.
  • Ignoring the cost of returns and refunds when calculating Black Friday performance. Sale periods often see return rates spike, sometimes doubling compared to full-price purchases, which erodes the revenue your ROAS calculation is based on. Track net revenue after returns when you evaluate campaign success, not just the gross figure your platform reports on the day of purchase.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good ROAS for Black Friday after accounting for discounts?

A good ROAS depends on your gross margin after the discount. If your margin drops from 40 percent to 20 percent during Black Friday, your break-even ROAS doubles. For example, if you need a ROAS of 2.5 to break even at full price, you will need a ROAS of 5 to break even during a 50-percent-off sale. Calculate your actual margin on each discounted product, then set your target ROAS high enough to cover ad spend, product cost, shipping, and any other variable costs.

How do I calculate break-even ROAS for a promotional campaign?

Divide one by your gross margin percentage (after the discount). If your margin is 25 percent, your break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4. This means you need £4 of revenue for every £1 spent on ads just to cover costs. Any ROAS below that number loses money, and any ROAS above it generates profit. Recalculate this figure for every discount level, because a deeper sale requires a higher ROAS to stay profitable.

Should I bid differently for new customers versus returning customers on Black Friday?

Yes. Returning customers usually have higher lifetime value and lower return rates, so you can afford to pay more to win their purchase even if the immediate ROAS looks lower. Segment your campaigns or audiences by customer type in Google Ads, then raise bids or set a lower target ROAS for your existing-customer segment. New bargain hunters acquired during the sale often never return, which means their long-term value is much lower and your bids should reflect that difference.

Does a high ROAS during a sale period guarantee profit?

No. ROAS measures revenue divided by ad spend, but it ignores product cost, discounts, shipping, returns, and the lifetime value of the customer you acquire. A ROAS of 5 can still lose money if your margin after the discount is only 15 percent and your break-even ROAS is 6.7. Track contribution margin (revenue minus all variable costs) and the twelve-month value of customers acquired during the sale to know whether your Black Friday campaigns actually made a profit.

Recap

Black Friday ROAS can look impressive on paper but hide losses if you ignore your actual margin after discounts, the cost of returns, or the difference between one-time bargain hunters and loyal repeat customers. A campaign that delivers a ROAS of 5 might still lose money if your break-even point after a 50-percent-off sale is 6.7. Track contribution margin, segment by customer type, and calculate your real break-even ROAS for every discount level so you know which sales actually made a profit. The highest ROAS is not always the most profitable result, especially during a promotional period when margins are thin and return rates spike.
